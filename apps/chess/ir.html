The Board — engineering design

Compiled from brief.md (pinned above) by the studio team, onto the mecha cluster, the omnishell terminal, the sayt loop and the bayt build graph. The visual direction is the design canvas the owner signed off, and DESIGN.md carries it.

Overview

A chess board with a clock, five opponents and several games standing at once, with no server side. Three entities take the device path so a game, its moves and the next game's terms outlive the tab; five take tab, because every one of them is a function of the position and a derivation is cheaper to recompute than to store (decision-01).

Three ideas carry this compile. A move is a row: Legal holds every move the position allows, each with the position it leads to, so playing one is copying a field and an illegal move is never drawn (decision-03). A piece is a row too, and it keeps its identity: the board is drawn twice over — a grid that takes the gestures and a layer of placed pieces above it — so a move moves one node and its travel is a transition rather than a redraw (decision-16). And time arrives as an input: a machine ticks a counter nothing else writes, and the referee reads how far it has moved since it last looked (decision-15).

The correctness authority is outside this repository. Perft counts for six standard positions are published and exact; the suite walks that tree through the app's own Legal rows, and two published games replay by name (decision-09).

A finished game can be reviewed on the same board: the engine reads each of its positions, and every move is marked by a rule over those evaluations that is written down once (decision-24, decision-25). The reading is a question the board puts one position at a time, and the moves' own rows are its progress (decision-26).

Entities

Game — device

One sitting at the board: the position as a FEN, whose move it is, both clocks, the time control and the opponent, which colour the guest holds, what is being looked at, and how it ended. It is the row every region on the board resolves from, and it carries a great many derived columns — the two seats, the captures, the opening, the PGN — for one reason, stated once here: a template binds fields and cannot compute one. Every column below that looks like it could be worked out from another is a column some piece of markup binds directly (decision-17).

current is which game the board shows, and it is a separate flag from status because several boards stand at once: asking for another does not end this one, it hands the board over (decision-05, decision-18).

The next game's terms, apart from any game: one row, next. Move — device

One move of one game, written the way a score sheet writes it, carrying the position it reached and both clocks as they stood after it. Three readers want different halves: the sheet wants san and number, the repetition rule wants the position it reached, and a takeback wants the clocks back. The record fields are never rewritten — a move taken back is retracted, not removed (decision-07). The review fields — before, second, best, line, after, mark and tide — start empty, and are filled once, in order, by the reading (decision-26).

Square — tab

The sixty-four cells the board is drawn from, and what each is doing — the shading, the coordinate it prints, whether it is picked, a target, part of the last move, or the checked king's square. It is a projection of the position being LOOKED AT, which is the live one unless the reader has stepped back through the sheet.

It carries no piece any more. The grid takes the gestures and the layer above it holds the pieces, because a cell cannot travel (decision-16).

Piece — tab

One row per piece on the board, and the row keeps its identity across a move. That is the whole reason this table exists: the terminal keys an item node by its row id, so a piece whose row survives keeps its node, and a node that keeps its place in the tree can have its transform transitioned rather than being destroyed on one square and created on another (decision-16).

Identity is recovered rather than tracked. Each piece the position wants is matched to the nearest standing piece of the same kind and colour, so one move moves one node, a capture leaves one node unmatched, and stepping back through the sheet reassigns the whole set without the referee having to know it was a step and not a move.

Tick — tab

One row, one number, and one writer. A reduce runs in a compartment with nothing endowed and therefore has no clock, so time arrives the way randomness already does — as an input somebody else states. A machine on the board advances this every second and nothing else touches it; the referee reads how far it has moved since it last looked, so a beat the tab slept through costs the reader no time (decision-15).

Screens

board — the board

Reads: Game (filter current=eq.yes, order ordinal.desc) as a list of one, for the position, the seats, the clocks and how it is looked at; Setup (filter id=eq.next) for the setup card's four pickers; Square (order ord.asc) for the grid; Piece (order id.asc) for the layer above it; Legal (filter game_id=eq.{id}&ply=eq.{ply}&from_sq=eq.{selected}) for the moves of the piece picked up; Move (filter game_id=eq.{id}&retracted=eq.no, order ply.asc, projecting here against the cursor) for the sheet; Tick and Opening as whole tables the referee reads but nothing renders. The game read is a list of one rather than a singleton for the two structural reasons the terminal imposes: a singleton region hydrates no nested region, and it drives no screen state.

Mutations: one reduce, seven machines and five forms. The pickers are component-generated machines: four on the setup card New game opens, over the Setup row, and two beside the game's actions — the pieces and the board's turn — over the game row (decision-31); the four nav buttons are forms writing step, which the arrow keys submit too (decision-20); everything else is the referee's — a tap or a drag says which squares it touched, and what follows is read off the position. The metronome is its own machine over its own row and touches nothing else (decision-15). keep: 1 — the position is durable, so coming back from the shelf repaints the board it left.

shelf — every game, in progress or finished

Reads: Game twice — once filtered status=eq.playing for the games in progress, once status=eq.over for the finished ones — both ordered ordinal.desc. Two regions rather than one list with a divider, because they are two different things to a reader: one is somewhere to go back to, the other is something to read.

Mutations: one reduce and one navigation. Resuming sets which board is current, and that is two writes rather than one — exactly one game is current, so the board being taken up and the board letting go are both stated (resume-game, decision-18). A finished game resumes the same way, from its Review button, because the board is where a finished game is reviewed (decision-24): it lands on the board over, or reviewed when the house has already read it. The paths below name those board states as board:over and so on, because the walk crosses from this screen to that one. The position on each card in progress is drawn by a renderer from the game's own FEN (decision-21).

sheet — one game, written out

Reads: Game (filter id=eq.{param.id}) for the heading, the result, the PGN and the final position; Move (filter game_id=eq.{param.id}&retracted=eq.no, order ply.asc) for the moves and the clock beside each. Both take the route's own parameter, which is what makes a sheet a place rather than a mode of the shelf.

Mutations: none. keep: 0 — the route is parametrised, so a held instance would accumulate one screen per game ever opened. strip: false — it is reached from a row, never from everywhere.

The referee, and the metronome

referee — the rules, as a reduce

The engine the declarative surface cannot hold: move generation for six piece kinds, king safety, castling rights, en passant, promotion, the naming of a move, the five ways a game ends and the sixth the clock adds, the two seats, the captures, the opening, the PGN, and the house's reply. It is a Jessie module in an SES compartment — rows in, updates out, no DOM, no clock of its own, no randomness.

It sits in a seat the terminal already has: it is the board's data-on-mutation reduce, its data-on-click reduce for the squares and the controls, and its data-handler for the drag. One module answers a tap, a drag, a wake and a beat, which is why all four go the same way (decision-08).

  • It writes only rows. Its whole output is an update list. There is no path from it to the screen that does not go through the store (decision-02).
  • It is total and deterministic. The one draw a sitting needs is asked for and injected; the house's choice is a fixed alpha-beta search whose ties break on generation order, so the same position always yields the same move (decision-08).
  • It converges. Every derived write is compared against the row already there and skipped when equal, so the mutation that wakes it is one it did not cause (decision-04).
  • Its rules are checkable without the app. The Legal rows are the whole of what it knows about chess, and they are a pure function of one FEN string (decision-09).

metronome — the beat, and nothing else

A one-line Jessie module named by a machine's assign: given the beat, the next one. It is the only thing in the app that knows time has passed, and it does not know how much — the referee reads the difference between two readings, so a beat the tab slept through costs the reader nothing.

It exists because the platform's two write vocabularies have different powers. A reduce can ask the terminal for a draw and for a wait, but not for the time; a machine's after is a wait the terminal performs and re-arms. So the clock is a machine over a row nothing else writes, and the referee is a reader of it — which keeps one writer per row and makes the whole thing replayable under ?tempo= (decision-15).

resume — which board you are at

Fifteen lines, and it exists because exactly one game may be current. Resuming is two writes and not one: the game asked for takes the board, and whatever held it lets go. A form could state the first and not the second — an upsert says what a row should be, and it cannot also say what a different row should stop being — and the store enforces the partial unique either way, so a single write would simply be refused.

It is the smallest reduce in the tree and a fair example of when one is right: not because the logic is hard, but because the fact is about two rows (decision-18).

The engine, held at arm's length

stockfish — an oracle, seated in its own thread

The one vendored unit in this tree. It is Stockfish 18 as published on npm — a single-threaded WASM build, 7.3 MB of object code, pinned by sha256 — plus a wrapper of about eighty lines that this repository wrote and reviewed. The wrapper is the unit; the engine is what the wrapper loads.

It sits behind the terminal's worker boundary, and the honest description of that boundary is audited and pinned, never contained. A worker buys a thread and nothing else: it is same-origin, so fetch, IndexedDB and the cache API all survive, which is more ambient authority than the frame seat and not less. What stands behind the unit is that an engineer read the wrapper and that the bytes it loads are the bytes whose hash is recorded — not a sandbox. Containment and threading are different boundaries, and a search that blocked the board would have been useless however well contained.

The wrapper is where the engine is made reproducible, and it is configuration rather than a patch — no fork, no source edit. It fixes Threads 1 and Hash 16, sends ucinewgame before every search so no transposition table carries between positions, budgets in go nodes and never movetime, and never sends UCI_LimitStrength, whose Skill::pick_best holds a clock-seeded PRNG. What that costs is stated where it is paid: a cleared table is real playing strength given up, and the node budget is uncalibrated — nobody knows what a rung is worth in Elo.

The wrapper also refuses to hand the engine a line it has not vetted. A FEN arriving from a row is matched against a FEN grammar before it can become part of a position command, because this file is the only reader between a device row and a command parser.

What it may say, and how little that is. The unit's whole vocabulary at the seat is one event named answer, carrying four strings: the game, the position it was asked about, the ply, and a move in coordinate notation. While a finished game is being read it is asked for two lines and the answer carries three strings more — the score of the best line, the score of the second, and the best line itself in coordinate notation — seven keys under the terminal's eight-key grammar, and still nothing that is not a string (decision-26). The terminal parses that detail against a fixed grammar and rebuilds it frozen, so the unit's own object never reaches a reduce; the referee then applies the four layers of decision-22. It is a source in the graph and never an operator inside it: its move arrives as an event and is recorded as a Move row, which is what keeps every derived table a pure function of its inputs (decision-04).

Invariants & test pairs

The move generator agrees with the published perft counts. From each of the six standard positions, the number of leaf nodes of the legal-move tree at each depth is exactly the number the literature states — for the initial position output.counts == [20, 400, 8902, 197281] — and the tree is walked through the app's own Legal rows, so what is measured is what the board offers.

A published game replays move for move. The Opera Game and the Immortal Game are fed back in and matched against the legal rows by name alone: output.matchesPerMove.all(n, n == 1), and both end with output.termination == "checkmate".

Two pieces of a kind reaching one square are told apart by the smallest thing that tells them apart — the file, then the rank, then the whole square: output.byFile == "Nbd2" && output.byRank == "N1c3" && output.bySquare == "Qa1d4".

Castling is offered on a side exactly while the right stands, the squares between are empty, and the king neither stands in nor crosses an attacked square: output.bothSides == ["O-O", "O-O-O"] && !output.throughCheck.exists(m, m == "O-O").

The en passant capture is offered for exactly one move after the double step, and not when taking would leave the mover's own king attacked — output.offered && !output.whenPinned. The second half is the case a generator that filters king safety after the fact gets wrong.

A pawn reaching the far rank is asked rather than assumed. The gesture names four moves, so the board writes promo_from and promo_to and offers the figures; the answer is the move that gets played: output.asks && output.san == "a8=N" && output.promotions == 4 (decision-10).

Each ending is named apart, each names its result, and the board keeps the position it ended on: output.stalemate == "stalemate" && output.insufficient == "insufficient" && output.fifty == "fifty-move" && output.draws.all(r, r == "1/2-1/2").

A position reached three times ends the game as a draw, counted over the placement, the side to move, the castling rights and the en passant square — output.termination == "threefold" && output.countedFields == 4.

A move that gives check is named with a + and the checked king's square is marked: output.san.endsWith("+") && output.checkedSquare == output.kingSquare.

The clock counts down for the side to move, adds the increment after that side's move, and does not start until both sides have moved: output.beforePly2 == input.beforePly2 && output.spent == 5000 && output.afterMove == output.spent + 2000. Time arrives as beats of a row somebody else writes, so this is driven by moving the metronome rather than by waiting (decision-15).

A clock at zero ends the game, and it is a draw when the other side could never have mated: output.termination == "flag" && output.result == "0-1" && output.againstBareKing == "1/2-1/2". The second conjunct is the rule most implementations skip, and it is the one a player notices.

What each side has taken is what is missing from the other's army, and the difference is the material edge: output.takenWhite.size() > 0 && output.takenBlack.size() > 0 && output.edge == 2.

The opening is the longest named line the moves so far match: output.eco == "C60" && output.opening == "Ruy López".

Stepping through the sheet shows old positions, changes nothing, and one gesture returns to the game: output.shown == output.fenAfterPly2 && output.legalOffered == 0 && output.ply == input.plies. The middle conjunct is what makes review read-only: no legal rows are written, so there is nothing on the board to tap (decision-14).

Taking back against the house retracts both half-moves and restores the position and the clocks, and the sheet still holds them marked: output.ply == input.plies - 2 && output.retracted == 2 && output.rowsStillThere == input.plies && output.hotseatOffers == false.

The house answers a draw offer: it takes one when it is not better and declines when it is — output.evenAnswer == "agreement" && output.winningAnswer == "declined".

Every level answers within a beat, and a stronger one finds a mate in two that a weaker one does not: output.everyLevelMoves && output.strongFindsMate. The weakening is by rank rather than by noise, because an opponent that varies per draw cannot be held to an oracle at all (decision-08).

The two pickers for how a game is looked at — the pieces and which way round the board sits — stand in every state the exploration reaches and in a finished game under review, and a choice on either sticks through the wakes after it and shows: the board turned, a1's cell and its rook at the other corner, or the set the pieces are drawn in: output.unreachable == 0 && output.lost == 0 && output.unshown == 0.

Resigning ends the game at once and gives it to the other side: output.status == "over" && output.termination == "resignation" && output.result != "1/2-1/2".

Handing both sides over does not disturb the position: output.fen == input.fen && output.ply == input.ply && output.movableSides == 2.

The game outlives the tab and the app owes the network nothing: output.fen == input.fen && output.requestsDuringGame == 0. The second conjunct is why no webfont is fetched: the type is what the device already has (decision-11).

The shelf lists every game newest first, says so when there are none, and a row opens a sheet carrying the moves, the PGN and the final position: output.ordered && output.sheetMoves == input.plies && output.pgn.startsWith("1.") && output.fen.size() > 20.

Turning the board puts black at the bottom, both coordinate strips agree with it, and nothing on the screen evaluates the position: output.bottomRank == 1 && output.evaluationElements == 0.

Every target is at least a thumb wide, the only motion in the app is a piece travelling, and both appearances render: output.smallestTarget >= input.minSize && output.loopingAnimations == 0 && output.reducedMotionTransitions == 0.

The marks are the rule applied to the evaluations and nothing else. The accuracies are stated exactly, so a formula that drifted would fail here rather than print a plausible number. The engine is not in this test — the rule is a function of numbers the rows already hold (decision-25).

A finished game is read one position at a time, each question asking for two lines, and ends with both sides' accuracy; a game still being played puts no question at all: output.questions == input.plies + 1 && output.lines.all(l, l == "2") && output.review == "read" && output.accuracies.size() == 2 && output.askedWhilePlaying == 0. An answer about this position that the board cannot use — a move the position has not got, or no exact score — writes no move: it raises the question's node budget by one. The seat never puts the same question twice, so that makes it a new question, and the reading cannot wait on it forever.

On a marked move the note names the house's preference in notation and the arrow runs between that move's squares as the board is drawn: output.note.contains(output.best) && output.arrowFrom == output.bestFrom && output.arrowTo == output.bestTo. Replaying real games through the referee, each move changes the square of the piece that moved and of no other, and castling moves exactly two: output.wrongTravels == 0 && output.castlingTravels.all(n, n == 2). A row that changes square is a node that travels, so this is the animation, asserted without a browser (decision-29).

Every member of the cast is seated with the rating the bench measured for it, read from the bench's own result file: output.ratedMembers == 6 && output.everyRatingFromBench. A rating typed by hand would fail here, which is the point (decision-30).

No screen's text names how the app is built — a stated list of the words that did (device, row, standing, written down…) is searched for in every screen's markup: output.developerWords == 0.

In the running stack, at 1280×800, 1440×900 and a short 1024×600, a game played to its end and reviewed never makes the page taller than the window, keeps the step buttons and the panel's foot in view, and stands the chances bar beside the board: output.pageHeight <= input.viewportHeight && output.stepButtonsInView && output.barBesideBoard. Layout is a browser's to decide, so this runs at integrate, against the launched stack (decision-27).

Decisions

01 — nothing leaves the device, and a game outlives the tab. The brief asks for a board that owes the network nothing and has nobody to sign in as, so durability is the only axis left to choose on. Game and Move are device: a game you were in the middle of is worth finding tomorrow, and a sheet is worth keeping. The other five are tab, because every one of them is a function of the position or of a beat, and storing a derivation durably is storing something that can go stale. The cost, stated: no cross-device anything. Making the same games follow a person to a second device is not a feature this tier can grow — it is the cluster tier plus a passkey, and it is the one thing in the brief this compile does not do.

02 — the referee is a reduce, not an app-owned module. truco reached for an app-owned script because its events were taps and the terminal's handler seat took only a drag; that seat now takes any declared DOM event, so this app's engine sits in it. The cost is that the engine cannot touch the DOM, so everything it decides has to become a row. What it buys is that the engine cannot touch the DOM — no imperative rendering to review — that it runs in a compartment with nothing endowed, and that it is a pure function the tests call directly.

03 — a move is a row, and the row carries the position it leads to. The alternative is a move being a function the screen calls with a from and a to. Then legality is a refusal — the board offers a move and takes it back — and the highlights are a second, separately-computed answer to the same question. Making Legal a table collapses both: the highlight is the row, playing the move is copying the position it carries, and an illegal move is never drawn.

04 — derived tables are recomputed absolutely and written differentially. The referee runs on data-on-mutation, so every write it makes wakes it again. It computes what the sixty-four cells, the thirty-two pieces and the legal set should be from the position alone, and emits only the rows that differ. Absolute recomputation keeps it idempotent; the differential write is what makes the fixpoint reachable at all.

05 — the board shows the current game, not the playing one. Filtering on status=eq.playing would clear the board at the exact moment a game ends, which is the moment its result is the only thing worth showing. So current is a separate flag, and it is also what lets several games be playing at once (decision-18).

06 — the position is a FEN, and the history is the moves. The three rules that are not about one move — repetition, the fifty-move count, and whose castling rights are whose — are all questions about the six FEN fields, and FEN is what the rest of chess reads. The sixty-four cells and the thirty-two pieces exist anyway, but downstream of it, and a projection can be wrong in only one direction.

07 — a reduce cannot delete, so nothing is deleted. A reduce's updates are put and patch; there is no remove. The first compile of this app took that as a wall and shipped no takeback. It is not a wall, it is a shape: a move taken back is retracted and filtered out of the sheet, and the record still holds it — which is a better answer than deletion, because a score sheet that can be silently rewritten is not a record. The same shape covers Legal (keyed by the move, filtered by ply) and Piece (a taken piece is marked so it can fade where it stood). The platform limit is real and it is still worth stating: a derived set on a crud tier would grow without bound.

08 — the house is a cast, and its weakening is by rank rather than by noise. A compartment has no randomness, so an opponent that plays "a random reasonable move" would have to ask the terminal for a draw on every move — and one that varies per draw cannot be held to an oracle at all. So the four levels are an alpha-beta search at one to four plies, and the weakest declines the best move by a stated rank. Ties break on generation order, so the same position always yields the same move. They have names and a line each because "level 2" is a slider and Nell is somebody to beat.

09 — the correctness authority is outside this repository. Perft counts for six standard positions are published, exact, and independent of any implementation. The suite walks the tree through the app's own Legal rows and compares, and two published games are replayed by name. This is the reason to compile this app at all: nearly every other brief is graded by its own acceptance list, which cannot catch a rule everybody involved misunderstood the same way.

10 — promotion asks. The first compile played a queen and left the other three in the table, on the argument that a picker is a whole modal vocabulary for a choice that is a queen ninety-nine times in a hundred. The hundredth is the one where a queen is stalemate, and a board that cannot underpromote is not a chess board. The picker is two columns on the game row and four buttons — no modal vocabulary was needed, because the board it covers is still the board.

11 — the type is what the device already has. The design canvas set an editorial pairing — a display serif, a grotesque, a monospace for the figures. Two of the three would be webfonts, and a webfont is a network request, which is the one thing this app promises not to make. So the stacks resolve out of the device: Georgia and its kin for the display face, the system UI face for text, ui-monospace for the clocks and the notation. The identity survives because it was never in the specific faces — it is paper, ink, tabular figures and a tinted board.

12 — the sheet carries PGN and FEN as text, not as a file. A download is a capability the terminal does not publish, and a "copy to clipboard" is a second write path to nothing. Both strings are on the page, selectable, and readable by any other board — which is what the interchange formats are for. Making them a file is a later decision with a real consumer behind it.

13 — no auth block, and what that costs. Nothing here is per-person: the device is the identity. The brief asks for one thing this decision refuses — the same game on a phone and on a desktop — and the honest answer is that it needs the cluster tier and a passkey, not a bigger local store. It is scoped out rather than approximated, because a "sync" that only works on one machine is worse than none.

14 — review is read-only by absence. While the cursor is on an old move the referee writes no Legal rows at all. So there is nothing to tap: the board does not guard the gesture, it simply has no moves on it. That is the same move as decision-03 read backwards — if a move is a row, then no rows is no moves — and it means the read-only state cannot be got wrong by forgetting a check.

15 — the clock is a machine over a row nothing else writes. A reduce has no clock: it can ask the terminal for a draw and for a wait, but not for the time. A machine's after is a wait the terminal performs and re-arms on a self-target, so the metronome is a machine advancing one column of one row, and the referee reads how far that number has moved since it last looked. One writer per row throughout, and three properties fall out: a beat the tab slept through costs no time, the whole clock replays under ?tempo=, and the test drives time by moving the counter instead of waiting. The first design had the referee tick its own column and lost the one-writer rule to do it.

16 — the board is drawn twice, and that is what makes a piece move. Square is the grid — shading, coordinates, dots, drag targets — and Piece is a layer of absolutely-placed nodes above it. A cell cannot travel: a move on a single-layer board is one cell emptying while another fills, and no amount of animation fixes that. A piece whose ROW survives a move keeps its node, and a node that keeps its place in the tree can have its transform transitioned. Identity is recovered by matching each wanted piece to the nearest standing one of its kind, so the referee never has to know whether the position changed by a move or by a step through the sheet.

17 — a template binds fields, so the game row carries what the screen shows. The two seats, the captures, the material edge, the opening, the PGN and the piece figures are all derived, and all stored. The rule is the terminal's, not a preference: a template binds a column and cannot compute one, and the alternative — rendering both arrangements and hiding one — is two copies of a seat that will differ. The cost is a wide row; the check that keeps it honest is that every one of those columns is written differentially and compared, so a stale one is a bug the convergence test catches.

18 — several boards stand at once. Asking for a new game does not end the one before it: it hands over current, and the shelf is where you go back. That is one flag and a partial unique, and it turns the app from a board into a place with boards in it. Resuming is deliberately two writes — the game taken up and the one letting go — because exactly one row may say yes and the store enforces it.

19 — a game has an ordinal, not a date. The referee has no clock for stamping either (decision-15 gives it beats, not instants), so rather than a timestamp column that would always read as the epoch, the shelf orders on a number the referee counts. The cost is stated: the shelf says which game, never which day.

20 — the four nav buttons and the arrow keys write one command column. data-key binds a key to a form, and a form states a value; "the move before this one" is arithmetic a form cannot do. So the forms write step — first, back, forward, live — and the referee resolves it into a cursor and clears it. One path for two input modalities, which is the same argument as decision-08's single write path, and the reason a keyboard reader and a mouse reader cannot end up in different states.

21 — the shelf's thumbnails are a renderer. Sixty-four cells per standing game is not a column a template can bind and not worth sixty-four rows per game either. A renderer takes the FEN and returns the nodes; the node schema admits no style attribute, which is exactly why the colours stay in the screen's stylesheet where the design tokens already live. It is the first use in this tree of a renderer for structure rather than for prose.

22 — one escape hatch, and four layers under it. The tree declares exactly one: the stockfish unit, behind the terminal's worker boundary. That boundary is a thread and not a containment — same origin, so fetch, IndexedDB and the cache API survive — so the unit is audited and pinned, never contained, and nothing below assumes otherwise. The engine is trusted for a preference and never for a possibility, which is what makes an unread binary safe to seat: (1) the boundary is a parser — the terminal rebuilds the answer's detail from validated strings and freezes it, and the referee then matches the move against /^[a-h][1-8][a-h][1-8][qrbn]?$/ before reading it; (2) it is looked up and never interpreted — the move must be one the referee itself named for this position, so an illegal move has no row to be (decision-03); (3) it is epoched against the game and the FEN it was asked about, never the ply, because a takeback rewinds the ply to a different position and several games stand at once; (4) it is never load-bearing for correctness — a cast member states its seat, and an engine-seated one is answered by its unit or not at all, so no Move row can ever name somebody who did not choose it. No answer is a board that waits, which is a liveness cost and not a correctness one. Owed: nothing times that wait out yet, so a wedged unit stalls that game until the reader starts another. The other two pieces of app code are the referee and the metronome, and both still run in the terminal's own handler seat under the terminal's own isolation. GPLv3 rides with the binary and its duties are discharged in the served directory: the glue is vendored byte for byte so its banner survives as the §4 notice, and §6(d) is taken by serving LICENSE and NOTICE.md from the same place as the object code. The emitted bayt.cue redeclares the runtime trio unchanged.

23 — a drop states an intent, because the drag seat is not the reduce seat. The terminal's drag machinery calls a reduce with {items} alone — never the reads the region declared — and applies only {id, patch} updates to the region's own table. It is a reorder seat: enough to move a row up a list, and structurally unable to decide a chess move, which needs the whole world. So a drop writes drag_from on the square it landed on, and the referee resolves it on the wake that follows and clears it in the same change set. Two gestures, one answer, and the drag path costs one column. The finding worth carrying up is that the two seats look alike and are not: one gets the world and one gets a list.

24 — a review is the board looking back at a finished game. The first sketch put the review on the sheet, beside the record. The board already shows any position of a game when the cursor moves (decision-14), and already has the move list, the arrow keys, the referee and the engine's seat; a review on the sheet would have been a second plate, a second cursor and a second seat, each a copy that would come to differ. So a review is what the board adds to a game that is over: the marks in its move list, a strip of winning chances, the two accuracies, and a note and an arrow for the move being looked at. The brief's "the board does not think for you" holds the way review holds read-only — by absence: every review column is empty while the game is being played, so there is nothing to render rather than something hidden. The cost, stated: a game is reviewed where it stands on the board, so reviewing an older one means taking it up from the shelf first; the sheet only prints the marks.

25 — a mark is arithmetic over the engine's two lines, and the rule is written down once. A move is graded by what it cost its own side in winning chances, read from the evaluation by the curve Lichess publishes, 50 + 50·(2/(1 + e^(−0.00368208·cp)) − 1), with a mate read as the edge of the scale. The mover's chances before are those of the house's best line from the position it moved in; after, those of the position it reached. A loss of 20 points or more is ??, 10 is ?, 5 is ?!. ! is the only move: the house's own preference, played where its second line would have lost 10 or more — any other move that held would mean two moves held. The house's own preference is never marked down: a drop between its reading of one position and the next is the horizon of its search, not the move's cost. A move whose sequence so far is a line of the opening book is book and is not graded. Each graded move scores 103.1668·e^(−0.04354·loss) − 3.1669, held to 0–100, and a side's accuracy is the mean of its plain and harmonic averages, so one blunder costs more than a scatter of slips. No brilliancy: !! needs a judgement about a sacrifice that no single evaluation makes the same way twice. Because the rule reads only evaluations the rows already hold, its test states the evaluations and needs no engine — the oracle is the arithmetic.

26 — reading is a question the board puts, one position at a time, and the rows are the progress. The engine's seat already asks whatever ask says; a finished game under review says the first position whose reading its moves do not yet hold, for two lines, at a fixed node budget from a cleared table. The answer is written onto the two moves it concerns — the evaluation of the one that reached the position, and the preference, second line and expected line of the one played from it — and the next question follows from the rows. Nothing else holds the progress, so a reading interrupted by a closed tab or a board left behind resumes where the rows say. An answer writes only move rows, so the move list carries the referee's seat as well: a mutation wake is a region's own read changing, and without a seat over move the next question would wait for the metronome's next beat. Every layer of decision-22 carries over: the answer must name the game and the position asked; its move is looked up in the referee's own legal moves before it is named, and its line is named move by move and cut at the first move the position does not have; a position with no moves in it is scored by the referee, never asked. The cost is time — a cleared table per position is slower than a warm one, and the reading takes as long as the game has positions — bought for a review that reads the same twice.

27 — on a laptop or a desktop, the board screen fits the window. A game played on a page that scrolls is a game whose controls leave the screen, and the review made it worse: its panel pushed the move list and the step buttons under the fold. So the screen is sized from the window rather than from its content. The board takes the height the window has left after the two player bars, capped by the column's width; the side panel is exactly as tall as the board column; inside it only the move list scrolls, and the step buttons and the game's actions are pinned at its foot. On a phone the board and the panel cannot stand side by side, so they stack and the page may scroll — but the board and its two players still fit together above the fold. In review, the chances stand as a bar down the left side of the board, where the eye already is, rather than as a strip in the panel.

28 — the strip becomes a rail, in CSS alone. The terminal owns the navigation's markup — one link per route, inserted before the element that holds the screens — and the app owns only its stylesheet. So the rail is the same markup restyled: the body becomes two columns, the navigation a column down the left edge, each route a stacked icon and label, and the app's mark a pseudo-element above them. The route titles are the rail's labels, which is why the board's route is called Play. Nothing in the terminal changes, and every other app keeps its strip.

29 — a piece keeps its identity where it stands. The layer of pieces recovers identity rather than tracking it (decision-16), and it did so greedily, in reading order: each square the position wanted took the nearest standing piece of its kind. A piece that had not moved could therefore be handed the square the moving piece arrived on, and the moving piece then took its place — two nodes travelling for one move, with both positions correct. A 52-move game replayed through the referee did this on four moves (both bishops on Bc4; a second knight or rook on Nh4, Rad1 and Rag8). The match is now two passes: every standing piece already on a square the position wants it on keeps that square first, and only the squares left over are matched to the pieces left over, nearest first. The test replays games and asserts that one piece row changes square per move, and two when castling.

30 — a bot's strength is measured, and Stockfish's Elo dial is the ruler, not the opponent. Stockfish can play at a set Elo (UCI_LimitStrength with UCI_Elo), and it was considered as the cast itself and declined for three reasons: its floor is 1320, stronger than the two weakest members; its weakening picks among moves with a clock-seeded generator, so the same position would not always get the same reply, which is the property the tests hold the cast to (decision-08, decision-22); and the dial is calibrated at a time control, not at a node budget. So the dial measures instead: each member plays a set of balanced openings with both colours against the others and against Stockfish at stated Elo levels, the ratings are fitted with those levels fixed, and the number the app prints is the fit rounded to fifty. A member that scores nine in ten against the strongest level it met is printed as that level with a plus, because a fit past the last anchor measures nothing. The bench is kept beside the tests (tests/elo), is re-run whenever a member of the cast changes, and the test holds each member's printed rating to its output. Two members whose ratings fall within one standard error of each other are one opponent, not two, because the picker would offer a difference nobody can feel; the weakest two measured that way and were merged into Nell, and a game stored against the one merged away plays on against her.

31 — a game's terms are fixed when it starts; the next game's are set on their own. Who plays the other side, which of the cast, the clock and the guest's colour were pickers bound to the game on the board, so they could stand only while that game had not begun. A guest playing Black never saw them, because the house moves at once; after an ending they stood again and wrote onto the finished game, whose headline then named the member it was lost to and whose seat, shelf row and sheet named another. So the terms have a row of their own, Setup next, on a card New game opens from any state; Start copies it onto the game it opens, and nothing writes a game's mode, opponent, level, clock or side after that. One writer is named rather than forgotten: the member migration of decision-30 renames a merged member. Switch sides, which changed ends mid-game, is gone: against the house it rewrote the record — "You (White)" on a game whose Black moves you made — and choosing a colour is now the card's, while which way round the board sits keeps its own live picker, which stands in every state, a review included. The pieces and which way round the board sits stay live on the game, because they are how a game is looked at, not what it is. A device entity carries no seed, so the referee writes Setup's first value — Otto, the guest White, 5+0 against the house — the first time it wakes without one, and the first game opens from it rather than from a draw. The test that holds this explores the board breadth-first over its gestures and the house's and the clock's events, and asserts at every state reached that the card is one gesture away, exactly one game is current and no game's terms have moved.
